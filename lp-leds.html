LP-Led Secondaries

The original secondary market: LPs selling fund stakes for liquidity, portfolio management, or strategy shifts. Slower-growing than GP-leds but the bedrock of the asset class — and where portfolio-underwriting skill matters most.

Process anatomy

  1. Preparation: seller (often via advisor) assembles the portfolio data pack; decides full-auction vs. targeted process.
  2. First round: buyers bid % of NAV at the reference date, by fund or for the whole portfolio.
  3. Exclusivity & confirmatory diligence: winner locks price, papers the purchase-and-sale agreement.
  4. Transfers: GP consents, ROFRs cleared, interim cash flows trued up, closings often roll fund-by-fund.

How secondary buyers underwrite an LP portfolio

  • Bottom-up: re-underwrite each portfolio company — entry marks vs. realistic exit values, leverage, growth durability.
  • Top-down: GP quality and track record, alignment, distribution pacing, unfunded exposure.
  • Return math: secondaries funds typically target mid-to-high teens net IRR / ~1.6–1.9x net with lower loss rates than buyout — the pitch to LPs is equity-like returns with credit-like loss rates and a shorter J-curve.

Why the market keeps growing (the structural argument)

  • Private markets AUM has compounded for two decades, but exit channels (IPO, M&A) haven't kept pace → a widening liquidity gap.
  • The denominator effect and LP overallocation cycles create recurring selling pressure.
  • GPs discovered the CV as a legitimate fourth exit path — and holding trophy assets longer is often genuinely value-maximizing.
  • Capital formation lags deal supply → the market stays buyer-favorable, unusual in private markets.

Recent activity worth knowing

  • Elite endowments became sellers: Yale’s reported multi-billion-dollar portfolio sale process and Harvard’s reported ~$1B sale (Lexington-led) marked a regime shift in 2025 — when the most sophisticated LPs use the secondary market for portfolio management, the stigma of selling is officially dead. Tax pressure on endowments accelerated it.
  • Pensions and sovereigns rebalancing: steady mega-portfolio supply as allocators manage overexposure from the 2020–21 commitment boom.
  • 40-Act and evergreen buyers: retail-accessible vehicles have become a persistent bid for diversified LP portfolios, changing who competes on large deals.

Interview questions — basic to advanced

BASICWhat is an LP-led secondary?

An existing LP sells its interest in a fund — the funded NAV plus the obligation to meet remaining unfunded commitments — to a secondary buyer, who steps into the LP’s seat. Priced as a percentage of NAV at a reference date. The GP isn’t the counterparty but must typically consent to the transfer.

BASICWhy do LPs sell at a discount — isn't that value-destructive?

Sometimes the discount is the price of liquidity they genuinely need (overallocation, cash needs, strategy shifts). Sometimes NAV itself is stale, so a “discount” is closer to fair value than it looks. And portfolio management has value: freeing capital from a 12-year-old tail fund to redeploy can beat holding to the bitter end. The discount is a fee for immediacy, not automatically a loss.

INTERMEDIATEWalk me through the mechanics of pricing at a reference date.

Bids are struck as % of NAV as of a reference date (say, 12/31). Between reference date and close, the fund keeps operating: capital calls the buyer funds get added to the price, distributions to the seller get netted against it. So the economics transfer as of the reference date even though closing happens months later — the buyer effectively owns the interim period.

INTERMEDIATEHow do unfunded commitments affect what a buyer will pay?

The buyer inherits the obligation to fund them, so large unfunded on a young fund is really a hybrid: part secondary, part blind-pool primary. Buyers underwrite the GP’s likely deployment quality and discount accordingly; some deals price funded NAV and unfunded separately. A tail-end fund with minimal unfunded prices on asset value alone, which is why vintage drives structure.

INTERMEDIATEWhat are transfer restrictions and ROFRs, and why do they matter?

The LPA typically requires GP consent to transfer and sometimes gives existing LPs or the GP a right of first refusal. Practically, this means the GP quietly curates its LP base — buyers invest in GP relationships partly to be the name that gets consented quickly. ROFR risk also chills auctions: nobody wants to do full diligence just to get topped by an insider matching their price.

ADVANCEDHow do you underwrite a 40-line diversified LP portfolio in three weeks?

Triage, not uniform depth: the top 10–15 positions usually drive 70%+ of value — model those bottom-up to exit. The mid-tail gets a calibrated top-down treatment (GP quality, sector marks vs. public comps, distribution pacing). The true tail gets a haircut assumption. Then portfolio-level analysis: vintage and sector concentration, unfunded pacing, and a distribution model that drives the IRR far more than any single asset. The skill is knowing where precision pays and where it doesn't.

ADVANCEDA seller's portfolio is 60% one mega-fund you know well and 40% funds you've never seen. How does that change your bid?

Mosaic advantage on the 60% — if you hold that fund elsewhere, you have differentiated marks and can bid tighter than competitors. The 40% unknown gets wider discounts plus diligence-cost consideration. Net effect: your bid is barbelled, and you might propose a structured solution — buy the known piece outright, price the rest with a deferral or earn-out — which often wins against a flat bid from a buyer treating everything equally.

ADVANCEDHow does the prevalence of subscription lines complicate LP-led underwriting?

Sub lines mean fund-level cash flows are managed: called capital lags deployment and NAV includes assets funded by the facility. The buyer must model the facility's repayment (a near-term call on capital), adjust for the IRR flattering it created, and understand that headline “unfunded” may understate true near-term funding needs. Stapling on a NAV facility adds another layer of structural leverage senior to the LP interest being bought.

Underwriting deep dive — the math and the structures

Where the return actually comes from. Buy at 90% of NAV and three things drive your outcome: (1) discount accretion — pull-to-NAV as assets realize; (2) NAV growth — the portfolio compounding after you buy; (3) distribution pacing — how fast cash comes back. The math is unforgiving about duration: buying at 90 and exiting at NAV in one year is an 11% IRR from the discount alone; the same discount realized over three years is under 4% annualized. That is why tail-end portfolios trade at deep discounts (~73% of NAV) — not because assets are bad, but because slow, uncertain timing destroys the annualized value of any discount — and why buyers model distribution schedules more obsessively than exit multiples.

Reference-date mechanics, precisely. Economics transfer as of the reference date: distributions after it reduce the purchase price dollar-for-dollar; capital calls after it are added at cost. Nuance interviews love: post-reference-date cash flows move at 100 cents, so they dilute your discount — a fund that distributes 20% of NAV between reference date and close just converted a fifth of your discounted purchase into a par transaction. Sophisticated buyers pick reference dates (and speed to close) with this in mind.

The unfunded question. NAV $60M, unfunded $40M, price 90: you wire $54M and inherit a $40M obligation — total exposure $94M, nearly half of it effectively a blind-pool primary bet on the GP’s next deals. Young funds are therefore hybrid underwrites (asset value plus GP re-underwrite); tail-end funds with minimal unfunded are pure asset plays. Subscription lines complicate it further: NAV is struck net of the facility, and its repayment is a near-term capital call the headline “unfunded” may understate.

Structures that bridge bid-ask. When flat bids can’t clear: deferrals (pay 70–80% at close, remainder in 12–24 months — seller financing that lifts buyer IRR while preserving the seller’s headline price); structured splits (buyer takes priority distributions to a threshold — say its capital plus an 8–10% return — then upside splits back toward the seller, letting a seller keep optionality on recovery); mosaic or barbelled bids (price the funds you know tightly, discount the rest, or carve the portfolio between buyers). LP-led is increasingly a structuring market, not just a pricing market.

Adverse selection and the information game. Sellers choose what to show you — and often lead with their most liquid, best-known names precisely because those minimize the blended discount. The defense is coverage: big buyers maintain pricing libraries on thousands of funds, refreshed every process, so any new portfolio is 80% pre-underwritten the day the teaser lands. That database — plus incumbency in the funds being sold — is the real moat in LP-led investing, and it’s why the same handful of firms see every large deal.
