MODULE 02

Private Equity Fund Mechanics

The fund structure

  • GP (general partner): the firm. Manages the fund, makes decisions, bears unlimited liability (in theory), commits typically 1–5% of the fund ("GP commit") for alignment.
  • LPs (limited partners): passive investors who commit capital, drawn down over time via capital calls during a ~5-year investment period.
  • Fund life: ~10 years plus extensions. Deploy years 1–5, harvest years 4–10.
  • LPA: the limited partnership agreement — governs fees, waterfall, key-person provisions, GP removal, and the LPAC (LP advisory committee, which rules on conflicts).

Fund economics: 2-and-20

  • Management fee: ~1.5–2.0% of committed capital during the investment period, usually stepping down to invested capital afterward. Pays salaries and rent.
  • Carried interest ("carry"): ~20% of profits above a hurdle / preferred return (typically 8%), the real prize.
  • European (whole-fund) waterfall: carry paid only after LPs receive all capital plus pref across the whole fund. LP-friendly.
  • American (deal-by-deal) waterfall: carry paid per realized deal, with clawback provisions. GP-friendly.

Performance metrics

MOIC = Total Value / Invested Capital (gross multiple, ignores time) TVPI = (Distributions + NAV) / Paid-in (total value to paid-in) DPI = Distributions / Paid-in (realized — what LPs care about now) RVPI = NAV / Paid-in (unrealized) IRR = discount rate where NPV of cash flows = 0 (time-weighted)
  • IRR vs. MOIC tension: IRR rewards speed, MOIC rewards magnitude. A 1.5x in 12 months is a ~50% IRR but a mediocre multiple. LPs increasingly focus on DPI — "you can't eat IRR."
  • J-curve: early fund-life returns look negative (fees drawn, assets held at cost) before appreciating. Secondaries buyers love mid-life funds because the J-curve is already behind them.
  • IRR games to know: subscription credit lines delay capital calls and inflate IRR; NAV loans pull distributions forward; recycling provisions redeploy early proceeds.
Interview favorite

"A fund shows a 25% net IRR but 1.3x DPI in year 8 — is it a good fund?" Be skeptical: high IRR with low realized distributions late in fund life suggests early wins were small, marks may be stale, and the IRR is riding on unrealized NAV. Ask about the subscription line, the pace of exits, and where the residual value sits.

The continuation-vehicle waterfall — how it differs

CV economics are the traditional waterfall with four systematic changes. Buyers negotiate all of them:

  • Reset basis: the waterfall restarts from the CV’s purchase price, not original cost. The GP’s old carry crystallizes at the transaction — and the core alignment ask is that it rolls into the CV rather than cashing out.
  • Lower fee, tighter basis: commonly ~1% on invested capital (vs. ~2% on committed in flagships) — the buyer is paying for asset management, not sourcing.
  • Tiered carry: e.g., 10% to a 1.75–2.0x gross hurdle, stepping to 15–20% above — roughly a quarter of recent CV deals included a premium tier. The GP earns flagship-level economics only for genuine outperformance on the reset basis.
  • Shorter duration, no recycling: 3–5 year horizons with limited extensions, defined follow-on capital, and typically no reinvestment rights.

Worked contrast: European vs. deal-by-deal (and the clawback)

Fund makes two $100M deals. Deal A returns $300M in year 3; Deal B returns $50M in year 6. Total: $350M on $200M — $150M profit, so total carry should be $30M (ignore pref for arithmetic clarity).

Deal-by-deal pays the GP earlier (better for GP retention economics), which is why LPs demand interim clawback tests, escrow accounts, and net-loss carryforward language. Know the phrase “net of tax” clawback — LPs rarely recover 100 cents.

Nuances you don’t see in the textbook

  • Catch-up percentage varies: 100% catch-up is common but 50% catch-ups exist — slowing the GP’s path to full carry and meaningfully improving LP economics in moderate-return scenarios.
  • Hard vs. soft hurdles: a soft hurdle (pref + catch-up) pays carry on all profit once cleared; a hard hurdle pays carry only on profit above the hurdle — no catch-up. Same 8% headline, very different GP economics.
  • Fee basis mechanics: the step-down from committed to invested capital after the investment period, plus rate reductions when a successor fund starts charging — LPs watch for “double-dipping” windows.
  • Fee offsets: portfolio-company fees (monitoring, transaction, director) now typically offset management fees 100% — a decade ago GPs kept a share; legacy funds still vary.
  • Subscription lines and IRR: delaying capital calls 6–12 months can add hundreds of bps to early IRR while leaving MOIC untouched — which is why sophisticated LPs ask for since-inception returns both with and without the facility.
  • NAV facilities: fund-level loans against portfolio value — used for follow-ons or accelerating distributions. Controversial when they add leverage LPs didn’t underwrite; ILPA now recommends LPAC consent and disclosure.
  • Recycling: rights to reinvest early proceeds (often capped at 100–120% of commitments) — extends deployment, defers DPI.
  • GP commit financing: a GP commit funded via fee waivers is cashless — alignment on paper, weaker in substance. Buyers and LPs increasingly ask how the commit is actually funded.
  • Key person & removal: key-person events typically suspend the investment period automatically; removal comes in for-cause (low threshold, misconduct) and no-fault (supermajority, often with a carry haircut) flavors.
  • Transfer restrictions & ROFRs: the LPA plumbing that makes every LP-led secondary a consent exercise — and gives GPs quiet control over who owns their fund.

Worked math: catch-up variants and hard hurdles

Fund distributes $200 on $100 contributed (after fees); pref is $8 (simple, for arithmetic). Profit = $100.

  • 100% catch-up, 20% carry: LP gets $8 pref; GP catch-up $2 (so GP holds 20% of the $10 distributed so far); remaining $90 splits 80/20. GP total = $20 — exactly 20% of profit.
  • 50% catch-up: the catch-up tranche splits 50/50 until the GP reaches 20% of profit-to-date — solve 0.5t = 0.2(8+t) → t ≈ $5.33, GP ≈ $2.67 in catch-up. With ample profit the GP still reaches $20 total — the slower catch-up only changes outcomes when profits run out mid-catch-up. That’s the trap: 50% catch-up protects LPs in moderate scenarios, not great ones.
  • Hard hurdle (no catch-up): carry applies only to profit above the pref: 20% × $92 = $18.40 vs. $20 — the GP permanently loses carry on the pref tranche. Same “8% hurdle” headline, structurally different economics.

Sub-line IRR mechanics, numerically: a fund that delays capital calls 12 months via its subscription line starts the LP’s IRR clock a year late. A deal returning 2.0x over 5 years from deployment shows ~14.9% IRR unlevered at the LP level — but if the LP’s cash was outstanding only 4 years, the same 2.0x prints ~18.9%. MOIC identical; IRR flattered ~400bps. This is why sophisticated LPs ask for returns with and without the facility — and why IRR comparisons across funds with different facility usage are quietly apples-to-oranges.
