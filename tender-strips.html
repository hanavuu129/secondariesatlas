TRANSACTION TYPES

Tender Offers & Strip Sales

The lighter-touch GP-led structures: tenders move LP interests without touching assets; strip sales move a proportional slice of assets without picking winners. Smaller than CVs by volume, but every secondaries professional needs both cold.

Tender offers — how they work

  1. GP (usually via an advisor) invites secondary buyers to bid for the right to offer liquidity to its LP base.
  2. Winning buyer sets a price — % of NAV — open to all LPs for an election window (weeks).
  3. LPs individually choose: sell some, all, or nothing. Oversubscription triggers proration against the buyer’s cap.
  4. Interests transfer; the fund itself continues unchanged. Often paired with a staple — a primary commitment to the GP’s next fund.

Low uptake is common — tenders routinely see single-digit to ~20% election rates, because LPs who like the fund simply hold. That optionality is the structure’s core feature: nobody is forced to do anything.

Market context & reported examples

  • Venture is tender country: VC funds rarely fit CVs (concentration limits, board dynamics), so liquidity-seeking VC platforms have leaned on tenders — with mixed success, as several large reported attempts (including by high-profile crossover and venture firms) stumbled on the NAV credibility gap.
  • Strip sales as DPI machines: reported examples include large venture and growth managers selling ~$1B-scale strips of portfolio positions to secondary consortia — partial liquidity that keeps the GP’s story (and upside) intact.
  • Staples under scrutiny: as fundraising stays hard, staple ratios have crept into more processes; disclosure to tendering LPs is the governance frontier.

Interview questions — basic to advanced

BASICWhat's the difference between a tender offer and a continuation vehicle?

In a tender, LP interests change hands but the fund itself is untouched — no asset moves, no new vehicle, the GP just facilitates a buyer offering liquidity to any LP who wants it. In a CV, assets are sold out of the old fund into a new one with reset terms. Tenders are lighter-touch: no asset-level pricing fight, usually no fairness opinion, and full optionality for every LP.

BASICWhat is a strip sale?

The fund sells a proportional slice — say 20% of every asset (or of a defined subset) — into a new vehicle backed by secondary capital. LPs get partial liquidity while keeping most of their upside, and because the strip is pro-rata, there’s no cherry-picking fight over which assets go.

INTERMEDIATEWhy would a GP choose a tender over a CV?

When the goal is LP liquidity rather than asset duration: the GP doesn’t need more time or capital for any specific company, but its LP base wants an exit ramp — common for older funds, or GPs mid-fundraise wanting to show responsiveness. Tenders are faster, cheaper, and conflict-light because the GP isn’t on both sides of an asset sale. The trade: no fresh capital enters the assets, and the GP’s economics aren’t reset.

INTERMEDIATEWhat is a staple, and how does a buyer actually price it?

A stapled tender pairs the secondary purchase with a commitment to the GP’s new flagship fund (e.g., “for every $3 of secondary, $1 of primary”). The buyer prices the package: if the primary commitment is worth less to them than par — blind pool, fees, J-curve — they recover it by bidding lower on the secondary leg. Sellers effectively subsidize the GP’s fundraise, which is why ILPA pushes for disclosure of staple terms to the LPs being tendered.

ADVANCEDWhy have VC tender offers repeatedly struggled while buyout tenders clear?

The NAV credibility gap. Venture marks embed illiquid, model-based valuations of businesses whose outcomes are power-law distributed — buyers demand 25–40% discounts to underwrite that uncertainty, while VC LPs anchor on carrying value and often prefer to wait for the next markup. Bid-ask stays wide, uptake disappoints. Several high-profile venture tender attempts in recent years cleared only partially or repriced downward for exactly this reason.

ADVANCEDIn a strip sale, where do the conflicts hide if the strip is pro-rata?

Three places: (1) pricing — a single blended price across a strip of assets can overweight the strong names to justify the total; (2) scope — “pro-rata” across a chosen subset is still a choice: excluding the two best assets makes it a curated portfolio wearing pro-rata clothes; (3) economics — the new vehicle’s fees/carry accrue to the same GP, so the sale terms set their own future compensation. Buyers respond by pricing asset-by-asset internally even when quoting a single number.

INTERMEDIATEHow does a buyer actually set the tender price?

Triangulation: underwrite the fund bottom-up to a target return (that gives a ceiling), read where comparable LP-led stakes in this manager trade (the market check), and estimate the uptake curve — price too low and nothing tenders, wasting the process; too high and returns die. The cap is the safety valve: a generous price with a firm cap converts uncertainty about uptake into a proration everyone understands.

INTERMEDIATEWhy do buyers accept fund-level-only diligence in tenders?

Because the bet is on the manager’s marks and pacing, not on re-underwriting companies — and because optionality cuts both ways: with no forced sale, the buyer can’t demand CV-grade access. The discipline is manager selection: tenders concentrate in GPs whose NAVs have a track record of realizing at or above carrying value. That’s also exactly why venture tenders struggle.

ADVANCEDModel this: fund NAV $400M, you offer 92% with a $60M cap, and history suggests 10–25% uptake. What are your outcomes?

At 10% uptake: $40M NAV tendered, all accepted (under cap), you deploy $36.8M — undersized but clean. At 25%: $100M tendered, cap buys $65.2M of NAV ($60M ÷ 0.92), proration ~65%, you deploy the full $60M. Your realistic case is a $37–60M deployment — which is the actual reason buyers negotiate minimum-uptake walk rights: the downside isn’t losing money, it’s spending a quarter’s diligence budget on a sub-scale ticket.

ADVANCEDHow should a staple be sized — what's the buyer's math on 'how much primary can I stomach'?

Start from the value gap: if the primary is worth ~85–90c on the dollar to you (fees, J-curve, blind pool), each staple dollar costs 10–15c. Then set the ratio so the total package still clears your hurdle: at a 4:1 staple the drag is small enough to hide in rounding; at 1:1 it can consume the entire secondary discount. The honest internal test: would we commit to this flagship on a standalone basis? If no, every staple dollar is a price cut wearing a costume — size it, disclose it (to your own IC), and bid accordingly.

ADVANCEDSame fund, two liquidity paths: a tender at 90 or an LP-led auction where stakes clear at 88. Why might both prices be 'right'?

Different products: the tender offers every LP a no-pressure option at a GP-blessed price with clean transfer mechanics — sellers pay nothing in process cost or signaling. The auction price reflects a specific seller's urgency, size, and negotiating position. The 2-point gap is the value of optionality and GP sponsorship. When the gap inverts (auction above tender), it usually means the tender was priced to protect the buyer's cap — or the staple is eating the price.

ADVANCEDIn a strip sale, how do you handle the unfunded commitments attached to the stripped assets?

Three choices, all negotiated: the strip vehicle assumes its pro-rata share of follow-on obligations (cleanest alignment — new capital supports the same assets); the main fund retains funding duty (buyer gets a cleaner ticket but the fund's LPs subsidize assets they partially sold); or a defined follow-on facility inside the strip with caps. Buyers underwrite the follow-on plan as part of price — a strip of capital-hungry assets without committed follow-on is a value trap.

INTERMEDIATEWhat does the transfer paperwork in a tender actually consist of?

Per tendering LP: a purchase-and-sale agreement (price, reps, 1446(f) certificates), a transfer agreement the GP countersigns (admitting the buyer as substitute LP), and updated fund records. Multiply by dozens of sellers and the closing is an assembly line — which is why tender buyers maintain standardized doc packages and why GPs prefer buyers who’ve done it before. Operational competence is an underrated moat.

ADVANCEDA GP proposes a strip of 20% of 'the portfolio excluding two assets held for near-term exit.' Legitimate or red flag?

Could be either — the test is verification and price. Legitimate version: those two assets genuinely have signed or imminent exits, so excluding them spares the buyer paying today for cash arriving next quarter. Red-flag version: 'near-term exit' is aspiration, and the exclusion quietly removes the portfolio's quality. Diligence: demand evidence (processes underway, IOIs), and price the remaining 20-asset strip on its own merits as if the excluded names never existed. If the math only works including halo from the excluded assets, walk.

Staple economics — a worked example

Buyer bids 90% of NAV for up to $90M of LP interests, stapled 3:1 — so it must also commit $30M of primary capital to the GP’s new flagship. If the buyer values that blind-pool commitment at, say, 85 cents on the dollar (fees, J-curve, uncertainty), the staple costs it $4.5M of value — 5% of the secondary. A rational buyer shades its tender bid by roughly that amount: the LPs tendering at “90” are effectively funding the GP’s fundraise with ~5 points of price. This is exactly why ILPA insists staple terms be disclosed to the LPs being tendered — the transfer is invisible unless someone does this arithmetic out loud.

Election mechanics — the fine print

Real tenders carry conditions both ways: buyers set minimum-uptake conditions (walk rights if too few LPs tender — diligence costs need a minimum ticket, and tiny stakes in a big fund aren’t worth the transfer friction) and caps with proration above them. GPs are expected to stay neutral in communications — a GP cheerleading the tender invites the question of what it’s getting (usually the staple). The information pack is fund-level (quarterly reports, capital-account statements), not deal-level QoE — buyers here are underwriting the GP’s marks and pacing, not re-diligencing companies, which is why tender pricing hugs recent NAV for quality managers and gaps for venture.

Strip mechanics — what actually moves

A strip sale transfers a percentage interest in a defined asset set into a new vehicle: the fund keeps the remainder, boards don’t change, and the GP typically manages the strip on fresh (reduced) economics. Pricing is negotiated asset-by-asset internally even when quoted as one blended number — and the two structural debates are scope (“pro-rata across a chosen subset” is still a choice; excluding the two best assets makes it a curated portfolio wearing pro-rata clothes) and go-forward economics (the GP is setting the price of assets whose future fees and carry it will earn). Strips suit venture and growth precisely because they generate DPI without forcing board changes, concentration-limit problems, or the single-asset scrutiny a CV would trigger.

Market landscape in depth

Tenders and strips are the flow business of GP-leds — smaller headlines than mega-CVs but steadier: tenders cluster in (1) venture and growth, where CVs fit poorly and reported large-platform attempts (with their pricing struggles) taught the market its NAV-credibility lesson; (2) mid-market buyout funds past year 8 whose GPs want LP goodwill heading into a raise — often stapled; and (3) increasingly, credit funds, where par-adjacent pricing makes elections easy. Strips have become the venture liquidity tool of choice — reported $1B-scale strips by major venture platforms — because they manufacture DPI without board disruption or single-asset scrutiny. The governance frontier for both remains disclosure: staple terms to tendering LPs, and scope honesty in “pro-rata” strips.

Technical corner

  • NAV roll-forward: tender prices are struck off a reference-date NAV; if a new quarter lands before the election closes, deals either roll the price to the new NAV or keep the old with a true-up — negotiated upfront, contested when marks jump.
  • Min/max architecture: buyer minimums (walk rights below a floor) and caps (proration above) bracket the deal; sophisticated processes disclose both so LPs can predict proration before electing.
  • Blended vs. asset-by-asset strip pricing: quoted as one number, built internally per asset — ask for the build-up and you learn what the buyer really thinks of each name.
  • Strip governance: the strip vehicle typically mirrors the main fund’s decisions (sell when it sells, pro-rata) — buyers negotiate protective consents only for divergence events (extensions, follow-ons beyond plan, affiliate deals).
