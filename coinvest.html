TRANSACTION TYPES

Co-Investments

Direct investment alongside a GP’s fund, typically fee-free — the fastest-growing corner of LP portfolios and the natural sibling of secondaries: same GP-relationship engine, single-asset instead of portfolio underwriting.

Mechanics

  • Why GPs offer it: to write bigger equity checks than the fund's concentration limits allow, to deepen LP relationships (a fundraising sweetener), and to reduce reliance on syndication.
  • Why LPs love it: fee drag on a 2/20 fund is enormous — co-invest at 0/0 can add 300–500bps of net return; plus deal selection and faster deployment.
  • Syndicated vs. co-underwriting: most co-invests are offered post-signing with 2–4 week windows (LP relies heavily on GP diligence). Co-underwriting means being in the deal pre-signing with real diligence access — harder, more valuable, what dedicated co-invest teams (HarbourVest, AlpInvest, Hamilton Lane, Lexington) build toward.
  • The adverse selection question: do GPs show LPs their worst deals? The empirical answer is mostly no — the bigger risk is concentration in big deals in hot sectors at peak vintage, since co-invest supply spikes exactly when deal sizes do.
  • What the buyer's job looks like: rapid re-underwriting of the GP's model (stress the base case, kill the synergies, check the debt package), reference the GP's history in the sector, negotiate governance (board observer, information rights, tag/drag), decide in days not months.

Where the market is

  • Structural growth: as deal sizes rebound (mega take-privates returned in 2025–26, including the largest LBO on record), sponsors increasingly rely on LP co-capital to fill equity checks — co-invest supply tracks deal size.
  • Institutionalization: dedicated co-invest funds at the big platforms (HarbourVest, AlpInvest, Hamilton Lane, Lexington, StepStone) have turned an LP perk into a standalone asset class with its own fundraising cycle.
  • Mid-life capital: structured/preferred co-investments into existing portfolio companies blur into GP-led secondaries — the same buyers increasingly do both, which is why recruiting for the two overlaps.

Interview questions — basic to advanced

BASICWhy do GPs give away co-investment for free?

It’s not charity — it’s currency. Co-invest lets the GP write a bigger equity check than fund concentration limits allow, keeps deals from being syndicated to rivals, and rewards LPs who commit to the next flagship. The “free” economics are the price of deal capacity and fundraising loyalty.

BASICWhy do LPs want it so badly?

Fee math: 2-and-20 drag costs several hundred basis points of net return; co-invest at 0/0 keeps all of it. Plus deal selection (choose your spots), faster deployment, and a closer look at how the GP actually operates — valuable diligence for the next fund commitment.

INTERMEDIATESyndicated co-invest vs. co-underwriting — what's the real difference for the investor?

Timing and information. Syndicated: post-signing, 2–4 week window, GP’s diligence pack — you’re underwriting the sponsor’s judgment as much as the asset. Co-underwriting: at the table pre-signing with primary diligence access, real influence on terms, sometimes a fee for the commitment risk — and a much bigger team requirement. Platforms graduate from the first to the second as they scale.

INTERMEDIATEWhat governance rights does a co-investor actually get?

Usually modest: board observer (not a seat), information rights, tag-along, and drag obligations; consent rights only on narrow items like affiliate transactions. You are structurally a passenger — which is why underwriting the driver (the GP’s track record in this exact sector and playbook) is most of the work.

ADVANCEDIs adverse selection in co-investment real? What does the evidence say?

The clean version — GPs dumping bad deals on LPs — is mostly not supported: offered deals perform roughly in line with fund deals. The subtle version is real: co-invest supply spikes when deals are biggest, sectors are hottest, and vintages are toppiest, because that’s when GPs need the most co-capital. So a passive co-invest program overweights exactly the moments to be cautious. The fix is discipline on pace and price, not suspicion of motive.

ADVANCEDHow would you evaluate a mid-life (structured) co-investment — fresh capital into an existing portfolio company?

It’s halfway to a single-asset secondary: known asset, incumbent sponsor, but new-money terms. Underwrite (1) use of proceeds — offense (M&A, growth) vs. defense (deleveraging, covenant relief) changes everything; (2) entry basis vs. the sponsor’s — you’re often buying at today’s mark, so the mark’s credibility is the deal; (3) structure — these frequently come as preferred with a floor return plus equity upside, so model the downside protection honestly; (4) alignment — is the sponsor writing new money alongside you, or just selling you dilution?

INTERMEDIATEWhat is a VCOC management-rights letter and why does it show up in co-invest closings?

US pension (ERISA) investors often need their vehicles to qualify as 'venture capital operating companies,' which requires genuine management rights in portfolio companies — so co-invest SPVs obtain a management-rights letter (information access, consultation rights) from the company. It’s boilerplate until it’s missing; knowing the term signals you’ve seen real closings.

INTERMEDIATEHow are co-invest SPVs actually structured?

Typically a Delaware LP/LLC per deal, managed by the sponsor’s affiliate: no management fee or carry for strategic LPs (reduced economics for others), expenses passed through with negotiated caps, transfer restrictions mirroring the main deal, and drag/tag aligned with the fund’s exit. The diligence points: expense-cap language, what happens on follow-ons (pro-rata rights or dilution), and governance if the fund and SPV ever disagree — they won’t, because the sponsor controls both, which is itself the point to understand.

ADVANCEDHow do you evaluate a co-investment TRACK RECORD — what are the attribution traps?

Trap one: cherry-picked denominators — demand every deal offered-and-done, not a highlights reel, and compare against the same GP’s fund deals in the same period. Trap two: vintage mix — co-invest supply spikes at market tops, so raw returns embed timing; look at performance relative to each deal's vintage. Trap three: selection vs. access — a program that saw 100 deals and did 12 has demonstrated judgment; one that did all 12 it was shown has demonstrated relationships. Both can be fine; they’re different products.

ADVANCEDA GP offers your fund co-invest in a deal where its OLD fund is selling to its NEW fund. What's your framework?

This is a cross-fund transaction wearing a co-invest suit — underwrite it like a GP-led: (1) price validation — who set it, was there a competitive process or third-party lead; (2) incentive map — the GP sits on both sides, so whose carry crystallizes and whose basis resets; (3) your role — are you validating capital giving the price legitimacy, and are you being paid for that role; (4) governance — LPAC treatment on both funds. Plenty of these deals are fine; none of them deserve the light-touch diligence of a normal syndicated co-invest.

ADVANCEDDesign a co-invest pacing policy for a $1B program. What are the parameters that matter?

Annual deployment bands (say $150–250M) with authority to pause — not stop — in frothy markets; single-deal caps (5–7% of program) and single-GP caps (20–25%) so relationships don’t become concentrations; vintage-spreading rules that force partial participation across years rather than lumping; sector guardrails tied to where supply spikes (tech, healthcare); and a pre-cleared fast-track IC for syndicated windows with full IC reserved for co-underwrites. The policy’s job is to protect you from your own deal flow in 2021-style years.

INTERMEDIATEWhy do currency and structuring matter more in co-invest than people expect?

A euro-denominated deal held five years can move net returns more than the entry multiple debate; programs decide hedge-or-not as policy, not per deal. Structure-wise, non-US deals arrive through blockers or feeders with tax leakage that hits YOUR net even at 0/0 economics — 'no fee no carry' does not mean 'no friction.' Reading the structure chart is part of the underwrite.

ADVANCEDWhen is DECLINING a co-invest the right relationship move?

When your honest underwrite fails — and you say so with specificity and speed. GPs remember fast, reasoned declines ('price assumes multiple expansion we can’t underwrite') far more favorably than slow maybes that jam their syndication. Programs that decline well keep their allocation; programs that never decline reveal they’re not underwriting at all — and sophisticated GPs quietly downgrade capital that behaves like a rubber stamp.

ADVANCEDHow does a mid-life co-invest differ from participating in the original deal, mechanically?

Entry basis is today’s mark, not original cost — so the sponsor’s embedded gain is your starting valuation risk; capital structure has aged (existing leverage, maybe accrued preferred ahead of you); your instrument is often preferred with a floor precisely because common at the new mark is unattractive; and the honest exit-timeline question is shorter but the exit path may already be picked over. You’re underwriting the NEXT leg of value creation in isolation — which is exactly the single-asset-CV skill, just entered through a different door.

Allocation, expenses & the legal layer

Who gets the deal? Co-invest allocation is governed by the fund’s LPA, side letters, and the GP’s allocation policy — typically a priority ladder (strategic anchors and separate accounts first, then flagship LPs, then friends of the firm). Because allocation is discretionary and valuable, it’s a compliance topic: the SEC has repeatedly scrutinized whether allocation practices match disclosed policies. And note the fine print: co-invest is not always 0/0 — strategic LPs get no-fee/no-carry, but some GPs charge reduced economics (e.g., 1%/10%) to non-strategic participants or through dedicated co-invest vehicles.

Broken-deal expenses — the classic enforcement theme. When a deal dies, who eats the costs? Historically some GPs charged 100% of broken-deal expenses to the flagship fund even though co-investors would have shared the upside — an allocation the SEC has treated as a disclosure violation in enforcement actions. Today, expense-sharing mechanics for co-invest vehicles are standard diligence questions, and “how do you allocate broken-deal costs?” is a genuinely sharp question to ask a GP.

What the evidence says. Research on co-investment performance broadly finds offered deals perform in line with fund deals gross — the net advantage comes from fee savings, not deal selection magic. The real risk is portfolio-level: co-invest supply spikes with deal size and market heat, so an undisciplined program overweights the largest deals of the hottest vintages. Dispersion is also structurally higher — single deals, no portfolio effect — which is why sizing and pacing policies matter more than any individual underwrite.

The meta-irony worth knowing: dedicated co-investment funds charge their LPs roughly 1-and-10 to access deals that are free at the point of entry — the fee is for sourcing, selection, speed, and the GP relationships that generate allocation. That business model only works because allocation is scarce; scarcity is the product.

Market landscape in depth

Co-investment has industrialized: dedicated co-invest funds are a fixture of every mega-platform’s fundraising cycle, LPs treat allocation access as a negotiated term in primary commitments (side letters increasingly hard-code it), and the 2025–26 return of jumbo take-privates — the largest LBO on record among them — re-anchored the structural logic: equity checks that big require LP co-capital, full stop. Two live currents: mid-life/structured co-invest is converging with GP-led secondaries (the same buyers, the same single-asset skillset, preferred structures with floors), and fee creep at the edges — as demand for allocation outstrips supply, reduced-economics vehicles (1/10-style) are quietly expanding, testing how much of the “free” in co-invest was really scarcity pricing all along.

Technical corner

  • Per-deal SPVs: the standard wrapper — watch expense caps, follow-on rights, and transfer language mirroring the main deal.
  • VCOC letters: ERISA-driven management-rights letters from the portfolio company — routine, but their absence stalls pension money.
  • Broken-deal sharing: post-enforcement standard is co-invest vehicles bearing their share — confirm the mechanics, not just the principle.
  • Blockers and feeders: cross-border deals carry structural tax friction even at 0/0 — net-net matters, not headline economics.
  • Speed infrastructure: pre-cleared IC fast-tracks, standing legal templates, and 48-hour NDA turnarounds are how programs actually win allocation.
