DEAL CRAFT

Mock Case Studies

Six cases in the formats secondaries funds actually use, graded from analyst screen to principal round. Each has a time budget, data, questions with fully worked answers, what graders look for, and the traps. Every number is computed, not estimated — work them with a pen before you reveal.

LEVEL 1 · Analyst screen

Pricing a single LP interest

20 MIN
20:00

Setup

You are handed Meridian Buyout III, a 2017-vintage fund. Reference date 6/30: NAV $50.0M, unfunded $10.0M. The seller wants a bid as a percentage of NAV. The fund is past its hurdle, so incremental gains bear 20% carry (ignore loss netting). Management fees run $0.5M per year for years 1–4. Target net IRR: 15%.

Data

PositionNAVExit timingExit vs. NAVGross proceeds
Company A$25.0MYear 11.20x$30.0M
Company B$15.0MYear 31.50x$22.5M
Company C$10.0MYear 40.80x$8.0M
Unfunded $10M—Called yr 1, returned yr 51.60x on cost$16.0M

Questions

1. Build the net LP cash flows by year.
Year 1: A proceeds $30.0M less carry on the $5.0M gain ($1.0M) = $29.0M; fund the $10.0M call; fee $0.5M → net +$18.5M. Year 2: fee only → −$0.5M. Year 3: B $22.5M less carry $1.5M, less fee $0.5M → +$20.5M. Year 4: C $8.0M (loss, no carry) less fee $0.5M → +$7.5M. Year 5: unfunded returns $16.0M less carry on the $6.0M gain ($1.2M) → +$14.8M. Total net inflows $60.8 M against $50M of NAV.
2. What price (as %% of NAV) hits a 15%% net IRR?
Discount the flows at 15%: PV = $40.8 M = 81.7% of NAV. At 12% you could pay $43.9 M (87.8%); at 18% only $38.1 M (76.3%). Note how much the answer moves for three points of target return — that spread is the bid-ask in most LP auctions.
3. What is your MOIC at that price, and why is it low for a 15% IRR?
Net inflows $60.8 M on $40.8 M paid = 1.49x. Short duration: most cash returns in years 1 and 3, so a modest multiple still clears 15%. This is the discount-plus-duration engine of LP-led returns — and the trap: if timing slips, IRR collapses while MOIC barely moves.
4. Company A’s exit slips from year 1 to year 2. New price at 15%?
Year 1 becomes −$10.5M (call plus fee) and year 2 becomes +$28.5M. PV at 15% falls to $37.5 M (75.1% of NAV), a drop of 6.6 points purely from timing. Say it out loud: “exit timing is the biggest IRR driver in LP-led underwriting.”
5. Between the reference date and close the fund distributes $8.0M. What do you wire?
Price less interim distributions: $32.8 M. Your effective price on the remaining $42M of NAV is 78.2% — the distribution came back at 100 cents and diluted your discount. If the fund had called capital instead, you would add it at cost.

What graders look for

  • Clean year-by-year flows before any discounting — graders watch the order of operations.
  • Explicitly stating the 20% carry on gains and zero on losses.
  • Instinct that timing dominates: volunteering the slip sensitivity unprompted.
  • Knowing the reference-date true-up without being told.

Common traps

  • Discounting gross proceeds and forgetting carry and fees.
  • Treating the unfunded call as free (it is a negative flow in year 1).
  • Quoting price in dollars when the seller asked for % of NAV.
LEVEL 2 · Associate — portfolio

Triage and price a six-fund portfolio

35 MIN
35:00

Setup

A pension is selling a $380M-NAV portfolio of six funds in an advised auction. You have strategy-level pricing from your coverage database and 48 hours. The seller’s advisor signals the seller wants 90% of NAV. Your cost of capital for deferral math is 15%.

Data

FundNAV ($M)Your priceValue ($M)
Fund A — Buyout 201812092.0%110.4
Fund B — Buyout 20208095.0%76.0
Fund C — Growth 20216080.0%48.0
Fund D — Venture 20194070.0%28.0
Fund E — Credit 20225091.0%45.5
Fund F — Tail-end buyout 20133072.0%21.6
Total38086.7%329.5

Questions

1. Compute your blended bid and explain how you would allocate 48 hours.
NAV-weighted blend: 86.7% of NAV ($329.5M). Time allocation: Funds A and B are 53% of NAV — bottom-up on their top holdings; Fund E (credit) gets a yield-and-loss review; Funds C, D, F get calibrated top-down pricing from strategy curves and a hard look at marks. Triage is the skill being tested.
2. The seller wants 90%%. Bridge the gap with structure, not price.
Ask = $342.0M; your value = $329.5M; gap = $12.5M (3.3 points). Offer 90% headline with 20% deferred 18 months: the deferral is worth 0.20 × $342.0M × (1 − 1.15^−1.5) = $12.9M, so your economic price is $329.1M = 86.6% of NAV — essentially your underwriting. Headline price and economic price are different things.
3. The venture GP (Fund D) signals it will withhold consent. What happens to your bid?
Carve it out: ex-D NAV $340M, value $301.5M, blended 88.7% — the blend rises because you removed the lowest-priced line. The PSA should pre-agree carve-out pricing fund-by-fund so a failed consent adjusts price mechanically rather than reopening the deal.
4. Between reference date and close, Fund A distributes $15M and Fund C calls $5M. Wire amount?
Value $329.5M − $15M + $5M = $319.5M. Distributions reduce the price dollar-for-dollar; calls add at cost. Both move at 100 cents, so they dilute your blended discount slightly.
5. Fund E discloses a NAV facility at 15% LTV used to fund last quarter’s distribution. Adjust?
Yes. The facility is senior to the stake you are buying, its repayment comes out of the next distributions you were counting on, and “use of proceeds = distributions” means the fund pre-spent your DPI. Haircut Fund E’s price (several points), model repayment as a near-term call, and ask for covenant terms — this is the standard credit-committee question.

What graders look for

  • NAV-weighted math done without hesitation.
  • Deferral valued with a real discount rate and tenor, not hand-waved.
  • Carve-out logic and PSA mechanics.
  • Recognizing a NAV facility as structural leverage senior to the LP interest.

Common traps

  • Simple-averaging prices instead of NAV-weighting.
  • Treating deferrals as free money for the seller.
  • Forgetting the blend changes when a line drops out.
LEVEL 3 · Associate — GP-led

Lead a single-asset continuation vehicle

45 MIN
45:00

Setup

A healthcare-services platform (physician practice management) is being moved into a CV. LTM EBITDA $80M; net debt $320M. The GP’s reference-date mark is 13x EBITDA, implying NAV (equity) of $720M. Public comps trade at 11x; recent sponsor-to-sponsor deals cleared at 12x. The GP proposes 100% of NAV, 1.25% fee on committed capital, flat 20% carry over 8%, and will roll 60% of crystallized carry. Your target: 15–18% net IRR.

Data

Your underwritingAssumption
EBITDA growth10% p.a. → $128.8M in year 5
Exit multiple12.0x (at or below entry — never above comps)
Net debt at exit$200M (from $320M; $120M cumulative paydown)
Exit equity$1346.1M
Hold5 years

Questions

1. What does buying at par return?
Exit equity $1346.1M on $720.0M = 1.87x gross, 13.3% IRR. Below your 15% floor — before fees and carry. The GP’s 13x mark versus 11–12x comps is the whole problem: you would be paying a premium mark and modeling a lower exit multiple.
2. What price hits 18%%? And 15%%?
Discount exit equity at the hurdle: 18% → $588.4M = 81.7% of NAV; 15% → $669.2M = 93.0% of NAV. Your bid range is roughly 82–93 of NAV depending on how much conviction you have in the growth case — and whether structure can close the rest.
3. The GP will only accept 92%%. Show the buyer’s net return under market CV terms.
At 92% you invest $662.4M; gross exit $1346.1M. Apply tiered carry (10% to 2.0x gross, 17.5% above) → carry $70.0M; fee 1% on invested × 5 years = $33.1M. Net proceeds $1243.0M = 1.88x net, 13.4% IRR. Below 15%. So either accept lower conviction-adjusted returns, push price toward 85–88, or add a deferral (15% deferred 18 months lifts your IRR without touching headline price).
4. Counter the GP’s proposed terms.
Fee: 1.0%% on invested capital (committed-capital fees on a fully deployed CV are unjustifiable), step-down after year 4. Carry: tiered — 10%% over the 8%% pref, 15%% above 1.5x, 20%% above 2.0x — so full economics require outperformance on an asset the GP already knows. Alignment: 100%% rollover of crystallized carry (60%% is the red flag: cashing out 40%% of a “conviction” asset), fresh cash commitment, vesting over the hold, no fee on rolled GP capital. Governance: named key persons, consent rights on leverage and follow-ons, consent for any CV-of-CV.
5. Name the three diligence items that would change your answer.
(1) Fully loaded clinician compensation: healthcare-services EBITDA is only real after market-rate physician pay and expiring earn-outs. (2) The payer-contract calendar: which reimbursement contracts reprice during the hold, and the regulatory map (CPOM, FTC posture on roll-ups). (3) Organic versus acquired growth: strip out M&A to see same-store trends — if the 10%% is really multiple arbitrage, the exit multiple assumption fails too.

What graders look for

  • Instantly flagging the mark-versus-comps gap and refusing to model exit above entry.
  • Running the net return with real CV mechanics (tiers, fee basis), not gross numbers.
  • A counter on all three axes plus governance.
  • Sector-specific diligence rather than generic "check the model".

Common traps

  • Anchoring on NAV because "the GP knows the asset".
  • Modeling 13x exit because entry was 13x.
  • Accepting 60% carry rollover as alignment.
LEVEL 4 · Senior Associate — tender

Price a stapled tender offer

40 MIN
40:00

Setup

A mid-market buyout GP, fundraising Fund VI, invites tender bids on Fund IV ($600M NAV, year 8). It asks for a 3:1 staple into Fund VI. Your standalone underwriting of Fund IV supports 91.0% of NAV. Your primary team values a Fund VI commitment at 88c on the dollar (fees, J-curve, blind pool). You will cap the tender at $120M of purchase price. Uptake history for this GP’s peers: 10–35%.

Data

UptakeNAV tendered ($M)NAV you can buy ($M)Cash deployed ($M)Proration
10%60.060.052.2100.0%
20%120.0120.0104.4100.0%
35%210.0137.9120.065.7%

Questions

1. What tender price do you bid, and why is it below your standalone number?
Each $3 of secondary carries $1 of primary valued at 88c — a 12c shortfall per staple dollar, or 4.0 points per secondary dollar. Bid 91.0% standalone less 4.0 = 87.0% of NAV. The tendering LPs are funding the GP’s fundraise with those points, which is exactly why ILPA insists the staple be disclosed to them.
2. Walk through the three uptake scenarios.
At 10%: $60.0M tendered, all accepted (under cap), you deploy $52.2M and commit $17.4M of primary. At 20%: $120.0M tendered, still under cap, deploy $104.4M. At 35%: $210.0M tendered, the $120M cap buys only $137.9M of NAV, so every LP sells 65.7% of what it offered and you deploy the full $120M with a $40.0M primary commitment. Your realistic deployment is $52.2–120.0M — which is why you negotiate a minimum-uptake walk right.
3. The GP asks you to raise the price to 90 to "ensure a successful tender." Response?
Raising price raises uptake — LP selling is an upward-sloping supply curve — but the cap means more uptake just means more proration, not more deployment. If the GP wants higher uptake, the honest levers are a smaller staple ratio (4:1 lifts your bid by about 1 point), a lower staple altogether, or a larger cap. Do not pay for the GP’s fundraising twice.
4. Why does the information pack differ from a CV, and what does that imply for your bar?
Fund-level only — quarterly reports and capital accounts, not company QoE — because no asset is being sold and no LP is forced to act. Your bar is therefore manager selection: tenders work on GPs whose marks historically realize at or above carrying value. The staple then becomes a second underwrite: would you commit to Fund VI standalone? If not, every staple dollar is a disguised price cut.

What graders look for

  • The staple arithmetic, stated in points per secondary dollar.
  • Proration computed from buyable NAV = cap ÷ price, not cap alone.
  • Recognizing that price lifts uptake but the cap converts it to proration.
  • ILPA disclosure point raised unprompted.

Common traps

  • Pricing the staple at zero because "we like the GP".
  • Confusing cash deployed with NAV purchased.
  • Missing that a 4:1 ratio changes your bid.
LEVEL 5 · VP — structuring

Bridge a 7-point bid-ask with structure

50 MIN
50:00

Setup

A family office wants 95% of NAV for a $100M concentrated portfolio (three funds, one dominant asset). Your underwriting supports 88%. The seller will not move on headline price; it will consider structure. Your cost of capital is 15%; you believe the portfolio realizes between 0.9x and 1.6x of NAV over roughly three years.

Data

StructureMechanicsEconomic value to you
Deferral, 50% at 18 monthsPay 47.5 now, 47.5 later, unsecuredDeferral worth 9.0 pts → economic price 86.0
Deferral, 25% at 18 monthsPay 71.25 now, 23.75 laterWorth 4.5 pts → economic price 90.5
Earnout88 now + 7 contingent on realizing ≥ 1.3x NAVYou pay the 7 only in the scenario where you can afford it
Preferred structureFund 95; receive first distributions to 1.30x (123.5) then 15% of excessSee outcome table below

Questions

1. Quantify the deferral options against your 88.
A 50% deferral at 18 months is worth 9.0 points at your 15% cost of capital, taking the economic price to 86.0 — better than your underwriting, so you can give some back: a 25% deferral is worth 4.5 points (economic price 90.5), still inside your number if you believe the high case. Pair the 25% deferral with a 2-point earnout and you are at your 88 in the base case while the seller sees 95 on the term sheet.
2. Evaluate the preferred structure across outcomes.
Fund 95 as preferred with a 1.30x priority (123.5) then 15% of excess. Portfolio realizes 0.9x NAV (90): you receive 90.0 — a loss of 5.0; the pref did not protect you because the floor sits above the outcome. At 1.2x (120): you receive 120.0 (1.26x). At 1.6x (160): you receive 123.5 + 15% × 36.5 = 129.0 (1.36x) and the seller keeps 31.0. Lesson: a preferred structure protects the middle, caps the top, and does nothing in a true downside — so set the priority floor against the downside case, not the base case, or pair it with a smaller funded amount.
3. Which structure do you propose, and how do you present it to the seller?
Lead with the 25%% deferral plus earnout: it preserves the seller’s 95 headline, converts their price risk into modest credit risk on a well-capitalized buyer, and lands your economics at ~88 in the base case with upside sharing if the portfolio performs. Hold the preferred structure in reserve for a seller who truly believes in the recovery — it gives them 100%% of the upside above 1.3x in exchange for your seniority. Present both with the outcome table: sellers respond to seeing their own upside preserved.
4. What does the concentration in one dominant asset change?
Everything about the downside: portfolio math does not diversify a single name, so your 0.9x case may be 0.6x if that asset disappoints. That argues for the structures with real seniority (preferred with a floor set at 1.0x, or a smaller funded amount), tighter leakage and information covenants, and a hard look at whether you are being adversely selected — why is a sophisticated family office selling this now?

What graders look for

  • Deferral math with explicit rate and tenor.
  • Understanding exactly which scenarios a preferred structure protects.
  • A recommendation, not a menu — plus the reasoning for how to present it.
  • Adverse-selection awareness on concentrated sellers.

Common traps

  • Treating "95 with a deferral" as paying 95.
  • Assuming a 1.3x floor protects against losses.
  • Presenting structure jargon without the seller’s perspective.
LEVEL 6 · Principal round — judgment

A multi-asset CV with a bundling problem and a cross-fund angle

60 MIN
60:00

Setup

A sponsor proposes a four-asset CV out of Fund III: total NAV $900M. Three assets are strong (NAV $750M; you underwrite 1.8x over five years). The fourth (NAV $150M) is a troubled roll-up you underwrite at 0.6x. The GP wants par, 1.25%% fee on committed, flat 20%% carry, and its new Fund V to take 30%% of the CV. Fund III’s LPAC has asked the GP to justify the CV under the ILPA 2026 draft guidance. Your target is 17%% net.

Data

ScenarioExpected value yr 5 ($M)Gross MOIC at parPrice for 17%% IRR
All four assets at par ($900M)1440.01.60x$656.8M = 73.0% of NAV
Three strong assets only ($750M NAV)1350.01.80x$615.8M = 82.1% of NAV

Questions

1. Quantify the bundling problem.
Blended expected value $1440.0M on $900M = 1.60x at par — the weak asset drags a 1.8x portfolio to 1.60x. To earn 17%, you can pay only $656.8M for the bundle (73.0% of NAV) versus $615.8M for the three strong assets alone (82.1% of NAV). The weak asset costs the GP roughly 9 points of blended price — which is the number that makes the carve-out conversation rational for both sides.
2. Propose the transaction you would actually lead.
Carve the weak asset out (leave it in Fund III or price it separately at ~50%% with a dedicated plan) and lead a three-asset CV at ~82%% of NAV — or at a higher price if the GP accepts the full alignment package: 1.0%% fee on invested, tiered carry (10/15/20 at 1.5x and 2.0x), 100%% crystallized-carry rollover plus new cash, vesting, named key persons, consent rights on leverage and follow-ons, and consent for any CV-of-CV. If the GP insists on the bundle, price it as a bundle: 73 is the honest number, and let the LPAC see why.
3. How do you handle Fund V taking 30% of the CV?
Treat it as a cross-fund transaction inside the CV. Conditions: a third party (you) sets price and leads — Fund V is a price-taker, never the price-setter; Fund V’s participation is capped and disclosed to both LPACs; Fund V bears its pro-rata share of costs; and governance is yours, not the GP’s through its own fund. The benefit is real — the GP’s newer vintage has conviction — but only if the price is independently validated. Fund V LPs are owed the same disclosure as Fund III LPs.
4. The LPAC asks the GP to justify the CV against alternatives. Draft the memo’s logic.
Under the 2026 draft, the GP must show the CV serves existing LPs’ best interests despite the conflict and beats: (1) a traditional sale — why would three strong assets not clear in M&A? If they would, a sale may dominate; (2) a fund extension — cheaper but no liquidity and no new capital; (3) a NAV facility — provides DPI but levers the whole fund; (4) preferred equity — liquidity without a sale but expensive; (5) a strip — partial liquidity without choosing winners; (6) a tender — LP-level liquidity without touching assets; (7) a cross-fund sale to Fund V alone — fastest, but the weakest price validation. The CV wins only if the assets need both duration and new capital and a competitive process sets the price. The memo should also cover: disclosure template delivered, 30-day election window, status-quo or remain-in-place roll option, cost allocation, and the weak asset’s treatment.
5. Six years from now the GP proposes a CV-of-CV on the best asset. What did you negotiate today?
A consent right over any second continuation, a defined term with limited extensions, and exit-consultation rights. The underwriting answer today must include a credible non-CV exit path — if the only buyer in five years is another CV, the market is self-referential and your mark is unproven. Say the phrase: “we underwrite to a third-party clearing price.”

What graders look for

  • Turning the bundling instinct into a number (9 points of price).
  • A real counterproposal with every term and a rationale.
  • Treating Fund V participation as a conflict to structure, not a vote of confidence.
  • Fluency with the 2026 ILPA alternatives test.
  • Thinking two transactions ahead.

Common traps

  • Pricing the bundle at a blended discount without quantifying the carve-out.
  • Letting the GP’s Fund V set or influence price.
  • Reciting ILPA without applying it to this deal.
How to drill

Start the timer, write your answer to every question before opening any, then grade yourself against the rubric. Interviewers care about the order of operations and the sentence that frames each number far more than decimal precision. Repeat Case 3 until the counterproposal is reflexive.
