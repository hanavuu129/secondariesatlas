TRANSACTION TYPES

GP-Led Secondaries

GP-leds are transactions the sponsor initiates on its own assets — continuation vehicles above all. In H1 2026 they overtook LP-leds for the first time, making this the center of gravity of the entire secondary market.

LP-led vs. GP-led — orientation

LP-led (traditional)

  • An LP sells its existing fund interests (stakes + unfunded commitments) to a secondary buyer.
  • Motivations: liquidity needs, denominator effect, portfolio rebalancing, regulatory pressure.
  • Priced as a % of NAV at a reference date, adjusted for interim cash flows. Buyout funds often trade in the 90s%; venture meaningfully lower.
  • Buyer wins from: discount capture, J-curve mitigation, diversification, and buying blind-pool risk that has become known assets.

GP-led (the growth engine)

  • The GP initiates a transaction on its own assets — most commonly a continuation vehicle (CV).
  • Single-asset CV: the trophy asset the GP isn't ready to sell. Multi-asset CV: a strip or full remaining portfolio.
  • Also: tender offers (buyer offers to purchase LP stakes at a set price), strip sales, NAV-based facilities, fund restructurings.
  • Now roughly half the market by volume, up from a fraction a decade ago.

Continuation vehicle anatomy

  1. Setup: GP forms a new vehicle (the CV) to acquire asset(s) from its existing fund. Existing LPs choose to sell (take liquidity), roll (into the CV), or occasionally a "status quo" option.
  2. Pricing: a lead secondary buyer (or club) sets price through a competitive process run by an advisor; a fairness opinion is often obtained. ILPA guidance pushes for competitive processes, transparent conflict handling, and adequate LP decision windows.
  3. Alignment: the GP typically rolls 100% of its crystallized carry (or more) into the CV, plus new GP commitment. Buyers scrutinize this hard — it's the top signal.
  4. Terms negotiated: management fee on the CV (often lower than flagship), carry (frequently tiered — e.g., ratcheting up at 2.0x/2.5x MOIC), leakage between signing and closing, subscription facility use, carry escrow, LPAC composition, exclusivity, and follow-on capital for the asset.
  5. The conflict: the GP is on both sides — seller (as fiduciary to the old fund) and buyer (as manager of the CV). This is why process, price discovery, and LPAC approval matter so much, and why the SEC and ILPA keep publishing guidance.
Interview gold — "why would a GP do a CV instead of selling?"

Because the best asset in the fund is often the one they know best and want to hold longer — but the fund is out of time or capital. A CV lets the GP deliver liquidity to LPs who want it, keep managing the asset with fresh capital and reset economics, and avoid selling a compounder into a soft M&A market. Critics counter: it can also be a way to avoid a market price test and extend fee streams — which is exactly why buyers underwrite the asset like a new deal, not a rollover.

Interview questions — basic to advanced

BASICWhat is a continuation vehicle, in one minute?

A new fund the GP raises to buy one or more assets from its own existing fund. Existing LPs choose to sell (take cash) or roll into the new vehicle; a secondary buyer leads the pricing and anchors the new capital. The GP keeps managing the asset with a reset term, fresh follow-on capital, and renegotiated economics. It exists because the best asset in a fund is often the one the GP least wants to sell when the fund clock runs out.

BASICWhy would a GP do a CV instead of just selling the company?

Because a sale surrenders an asset they know intimately, often into a soft M&A market, while LPs still need liquidity today. The CV threads the needle: sellers get cash, the GP keeps the compounder, and the buyer gets a de-risked asset with an incumbent manager. The honest counterpoint — which interviewers want you to raise — is that CVs can also dodge a true market price test and extend fee streams, which is why process quality matters.

INTERMEDIATEWhat are the top alignment signals you'd check in a GP-led deal?

(1) GP rolling 100% of crystallized carry into the CV — the cleanest signal — plus fresh GP commitment on top. (2) Carry structure: tiered/ratcheted carry means the GP gets paid mostly for outperformance. (3) Management fee on invested (not committed) capital at a reduced rate. (4) Whether the GP took any chips off the table. A GP cashing out carry while asking buyers to re-underwrite the asset is the red flag.

INTERMEDIATEWalk me through how a single-asset CV gets priced.

The advisor runs a competitive process: buyers underwrite the company like a direct deal — model to exit, apply their return threshold — and bid a price usually expressed vs. the fund's carrying NAV at a reference date. The lead buyer sets terms; a syndicate may fill out the book. The LPAC reviews the conflict, ILPA guidance pushes for genuine price discovery and adequate election windows, and a fairness opinion is sometimes obtained — though these appeared in only ~11% of volume recently, meaning buyers increasingly rely on their own underwriting.

INTERMEDIATEWhat is leakage and why does it matter in CV negotiations?

Value leaving the asset between the pricing reference date and closing — dividends, fees, or expenses paid out to the seller side. In locked-box style pricing, the buyer pays for the asset as of the reference date, so any leakage after that date is value the buyer paid for but didn't receive. Negotiating leakage protections (and interest on the locked box) is standard deal mechanics.

ADVANCEDHow do purchase-price deferrals bridge bid-ask gaps, and what's the real cost?

The buyer pays, say, 90% at close and 10% in 12–24 months — the headline price looks full, but the deferred piece is effectively an interest-free loan from the seller, so the buyer's true economic price is lower and their IRR mechanically improves from delayed outflow. Deferrals appeared in roughly a quarter of recent continuation funds. For the seller, it converts price risk into counterparty/timing risk — which is why sellers scrutinize the buyer's funding and the conditions attached to the deferred payment.

ADVANCEDYou're underwriting a single-asset CV on a business the GP has owned for six years. What's different vs. a fresh buyout?

The information asymmetry inverts: the GP knows everything, you know the data room. So the diligence emphasis shifts to (1) why the upside remains — if the plan was so good, why wasn't it executed in the first hold; (2) entry basis vs. NAV — you're paying near carrying value, so the mark's credibility is the deal; (3) the GP's incentive shift with reset economics — a new carry ratchet on an already-marked-up basis; (4) exit path realism — who buys this in five years at a bigger number, given the sponsor universe has already passed on it once at this price.

ADVANCEDWhy has the fairness-opinion share of GP-led volume fallen, and is that good or bad?

It dropped to roughly 11% of volume — buyers and LPACs increasingly treat a competitive process with multiple bids as better price discovery than a paid opinion. Arguably healthy: it reflects a deeper, more institutional buyer universe doing fundamental underwriting. The risk case: in thin processes with one anchor buyer, removing the third-party check leaves LP protection resting entirely on LPAC sophistication — which varies widely.
