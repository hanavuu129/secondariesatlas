TRANSACTION TYPES

GP-Led Secondaries

GP-leds are transactions the sponsor initiates on its own assets — continuation vehicles above all. In H1 2026 they overtook LP-leds for the first time. This page goes deep: why every party shows up, the full deal anatomy with the legal debate points, the deals that defined the market, and the questions interviews are built from.

LP-led vs. GP-led — orientation

LP-led (traditional)

  • An LP sells its existing fund interests (stakes + unfunded commitments) to a secondary buyer.
  • Motivations: liquidity needs, denominator effect, portfolio rebalancing, regulatory pressure.
  • Priced as a % of NAV at a reference date, adjusted for interim cash flows. Buyout funds often trade in the 90s%; venture meaningfully lower.
  • Buyer wins from: discount capture, J-curve mitigation, diversification, and buying blind-pool risk that has become known assets.

GP-led (the growth engine)

  • The GP initiates a transaction on its own assets — most commonly a continuation vehicle (CV).
  • Single-asset CV: the trophy asset the GP isn't ready to sell. Multi-asset CV: a strip or full remaining portfolio.
  • Also: tender offers (buyer offers to purchase LP stakes at a set price), strip sales, NAV-based facilities, fund restructurings.
  • Now roughly half the market by volume, up from a fraction a decade ago.

Why LPs sell, roll — and what each side worries about

  • Selling LPs get liquidity at far better pricing than an LP-led sale of comparable exposure (single-asset CVs price ~95–100%+ of NAV vs. discounts on fund stakes), plus clean portfolio management. Concerns: compressed election windows (ILPA: 30+ days; deals have closed with less), information asymmetry (the GP knows the asset; the LP gets a data pack and a deadline), decision-under-duress framing (a “voluntary” choice between selling a winner and re-underwriting a new vehicle is still forced), and the releases GPs ask electing LPs to sign.
  • Rolling LPs keep exposure to a proven asset — but must check whether they’re paying carry twice on the same dollar of value (carry crystallizes at the CV, then new carry accrues from the reset basis), whether the roll is actually tax-deferred for them (often not, depending on structure), and whether a true status-quo option exists or only a roll on the CV’s new terms.
  • Buyers get a de-risked, information-rich asset with an incumbent manager and structural alignment. Concerns: paying the GP’s own mark (the NAV is set by the party selling to you), post-close incentive drift (the GP just monetized — is the hunger the same?), exit-path crowding (the CV-squared question), concentration limits forcing syndication, and process integrity — one anchor bid plus a price-taking syndicate is not price discovery.

Continuation vehicle anatomy — the practitioner version

1 · Process timeline (typically 4–6 months)

Pre-marketing: the advisor quietly tests appetite with 5–10 likely leads before anything formal — because a broken process is costly: if a CV launches, leaks, and fails, the asset is shopworn and the GP’s remaining options start from weakness. First round: pricing letters against a reference-date NAV, often with term grids (fee/carry/structure) so the GP can trade price against terms. Lead selection & exclusivity: the lead locks price and negotiates the real documents — purchase agreement (asset transfer), the CV’s LPA (go-forward economics and governance), and election materials. Election period: existing LPs choose sell / roll (ILPA: 30+ days; full disclosure of advisor fees, staples, and GP economics). Close: transfer, syndication fills, interim cash flows true up.

2 · Pricing architecture

Price is struck vs. NAV at a reference date — and the gap between that date and closing is where value moves. Most deals use a locked box: the buyer owns the economics from the reference date, protected by leakage covenants (no dividends, fees, or extractions to the seller side after the box closes), sometimes paying a ticking fee (interest on the equity price) for the delay. Watch stale reference dates: pricing a deal many months after the NAV was struck embeds unmarked performance — flattering or damning depending on direction, and a live debate on several landmark deals. Bid-ask bridges: deferrals (~25% of recent CVs — pay 80–90% at close, the rest in 12–24 months; headline price looks full while the buyer’s true cost is lower) and occasional earnouts tied to exit outcomes.

3 · Economics

Management fee: commonly ~1% on invested capital (vs. ~2% on committed in flagships) — buyers pay for asset management, not sourcing. Carry: tiered is now standard on quality deals — e.g., 10% to a 1.75–2.0x gross hurdle stepping to 15–20% above, with ~8% pref; roughly a quarter of recent deals included a premium tier. The alignment test buyers actually run: total GP exposure in the CV (rolled carry + new commitment) versus what the GP could have cashed out — 100% carry rollover plus fresh commitment is the clean answer. Also negotiated: organizational expense caps, broken-deal cost allocation, and who funds the process itself.

4 · Legal & governance — where the debates actually live

  • The double-agent problem: the GP owes duties to the selling fund (highest price) and controls the buying vehicle (lowest workable price). Everything below is machinery for managing that.
  • LPAC consent: clears the conflict contractually under the LPA — but doesn’t bless the price or extinguish every fiduciary theory; LPAC members also aren’t fiduciaries for other LPs.
  • Fairness opinions: down to ~11% of volume — the market treats a competitive multi-bid process as better price validation than a purchased opinion. Fine when processes are genuinely competitive; thin when they aren’t.
  • Regulatory state of play: the SEC’s 2023 Private Fund Adviser rules (which would have mandated fairness/valuation opinions for adviser-led secondaries) were vacated by the Fifth Circuit in June 2024. What governs today: Advisers Act antifraud principles, the LPA, and ILPA norms — not a federal opinion mandate.
  • Rollover tax mechanics: neither GP carry rolls nor LP rollovers are automatically tax-deferred; partnership contribution structuring and blockers (for tax-exempt and non-US rollers) are a real workstream.
  • RWI insurance: increasingly used so breach risk sits with an insurer rather than the selling fund’s LPs — a seller indemnity here is circular.
  • Lead-investor governance: beyond economics, leads negotiate consent rights (new leverage, follow-on sizing, affiliate transactions), key-person provisions tied to the specific partners running the asset, exit-consultation rights, and sometimes time-based sale triggers if the CV term lapses.

5 · Dynamics that make the market interesting

  • The syndication food chain: leads underwrite and set terms; syndicate capital accepts them on lighter diligence. Influence concentrates with whoever writes the anchor check — a structural reason big secondaries funds keep getting bigger.
  • Incumbent information edges: a buyer who already holds the GP’s funds (or led its last CV) prices with mosaic knowledge competitors lack — secondaries is a relationship-and-information flywheel.
  • Dedicated SACV pools: funds raised specifically to lead other sponsors’ single-asset deals (ICG Strategic Equity, AlpInvest, Lexington, Leonard Green’s Sage vehicle, H.I.G.’s effort) — single-asset underwriting has become its own asset class.
  • Performance evidence: the Evercore–HEC (Gottschalg) research found CVs on average performing competitively with buyout benchmarks with tighter dispersion — the empirical rebuttal to “CVs are where assets go to die.” Know it exists; don’t over-claim precision.
  • Pricing reality: per Lazard’s interim 2026 review, 87% of single-asset CV volume priced above 90% of NAV, a meaningful share at or above par. The market pays up for GP-selected quality — which is exactly why skeptics worry about the day that stops being true.

Recent transactions — deal-by-deal

Vista Equity / Cloud Software Group — $5.6B single-asset CV (2025)

The largest SACV on record, and unusually transparent: pricing was reported at ~5% discount to a NAV whose reference date sat well over a year before close — the canonical case study in the stale-reference-date debate (how much unmarked performance, in which direction, did the buyer inherit?). Rationale: an enormous enterprise-software platform (the Citrix take-private legacy) with a leverage-heavy history and more transformation runway than fund life. Debate points buyers argued: AI-disruption risk to mature software, the syndication scale required (no single fund could hold it), and what it proved — the CV channel can clear $5B+ single assets, effectively substituting for an IPO-scale exit.

Pantheon-led Crescent Credit Solutions VII CV — $3.2B (Jan 2026)

The largest credit continuation vehicle to date — the CV toolkit transplanted wholesale into private credit: duration extension on performing loan books, priced near fair value (GP-led credit ≈ 99% of FMV) because cash-yielding senior portfolios are underwriteable with far less variance than equity. The debate: whether credit CVs mostly solve portfolio needs or manager optics, given credit’s naturally shorter duration.

New Mountain / Real Chemistry — $3.1B SACV

Healthcare-marketing platform; classic trophy logic — proven organic-plus-M&A compounder, sector-convinced sponsor, more roadmap than fund. Part of the pattern where mega-SACVs concentrate in healthcare services and software: sectors where GPs claim durable, model-able growth. The skeptic’s angle: those are also the sectors where marks embed the most optimism.

Cerberus / SubCom — $2.3B SACV (2026)

Subsea cable systems — the only US-headquartered player — with demand supercharged by hyperscaler/AI interconnect buildouts. Interesting aspects: a national-security framing unusual in CVs (a strategic asset the sponsor argues shouldn’t change hands), and underwriting that turns on one question — are multi-year cable contracts infrastructure-like cash flows or a cyclical capex boom? That question drove the bid spread.

Ares / Convergint — $850M SACV (Mar 2026)

Security-integration services, sole-led by Leonard Green’s Sage vehicle — a fund built specifically to lead other sponsors’ single-asset CVs. The buyer archetype is the story. Debate point: sole-led processes and price discovery — with one underwriter and no competing bids at the end, integrity rests on the earlier rounds and LPAC scrutiny.

Carlyle Global Credit / Content Partners — credit SACV (Jun 2026; Moelis advised Carlyle)

A film-and-TV royalty library inside a credit CV: Carlyle’s Credit Opportunities Fund III participated as new capital alongside third parties while CCOF II holders elected liquidity or continuation. The fascinating part is the cross-fund conflict — the same manager’s newer vintage buying alongside/from its older vintage: conflict management squared, and a preview of how credit platforms will recycle assets across fund generations.

Montagu / Wireless Logic — €2B CV

IoT connectivity platform; emblematic of Europe’s surge (closed CV volume up ~93% YoY in Europe in 2025). Rationale: subscription-quality revenue with global runway — and a reminder that European deals bring their own flavor: different LP bases, AIFMD wrapper questions, currency considerations in syndication.

Details as publicly reported — treat sizes, pricing, and advisor roles as approximate, and never claim inside knowledge of deals you weren’t on.

Interview questions — basic to advanced

BASICWhat is a continuation vehicle, in one minute?

A new fund the GP raises to buy one or more assets from its own existing fund. Existing LPs choose to sell (take cash) or roll into the new vehicle; a secondary buyer leads the pricing and anchors the new capital. The GP keeps managing the asset with a reset term, fresh follow-on capital, and renegotiated economics. It exists because the best asset in a fund is often the one the GP least wants to sell when the fund clock runs out.

BASICWhy would a GP do a CV instead of just selling the company?

Because a sale surrenders an asset they know intimately, often into a soft M&A market, while LPs still need liquidity today. The CV threads the needle: sellers get cash, the GP keeps the compounder, and the buyer gets a de-risked asset with an incumbent manager. The honest counterpoint — which interviewers want you to raise — is that CVs can also dodge a true market price test and extend fee streams, which is why process quality matters.

INTERMEDIATEWhat are the top alignment signals you'd check in a GP-led deal?

(1) GP rolling 100% of crystallized carry into the CV — the cleanest signal — plus fresh GP commitment on top. (2) Carry structure: tiered/ratcheted carry means the GP gets paid mostly for outperformance. (3) Management fee on invested (not committed) capital at a reduced rate. (4) Whether the GP took any chips off the table. A GP cashing out carry while asking buyers to re-underwrite the asset is the red flag.

INTERMEDIATEWalk me through how a single-asset CV gets priced.

The advisor runs a competitive process: buyers underwrite the company like a direct deal — model to exit, apply their return threshold — and bid a price usually expressed vs. the fund's carrying NAV at a reference date. The lead buyer sets terms; a syndicate may fill out the book. The LPAC reviews the conflict, ILPA guidance pushes for genuine price discovery and adequate election windows, and a fairness opinion is sometimes obtained — though these appeared in only ~11% of volume recently, meaning buyers increasingly rely on their own underwriting.

INTERMEDIATEWhat is leakage and why does it matter in CV negotiations?

Value leaving the asset between the pricing reference date and closing — dividends, fees, or expenses paid out to the seller side. In locked-box style pricing, the buyer pays for the asset as of the reference date, so any leakage after that date is value the buyer paid for but didn't receive. Negotiating leakage protections (and interest on the locked box) is standard deal mechanics.

ADVANCEDHow do purchase-price deferrals bridge bid-ask gaps, and what's the real cost?

The buyer pays, say, 90% at close and 10% in 12–24 months — the headline price looks full, but the deferred piece is effectively an interest-free loan from the seller, so the buyer's true economic price is lower and their IRR mechanically improves from delayed outflow. Deferrals appeared in roughly a quarter of recent continuation funds. For the seller, it converts price risk into counterparty/timing risk — which is why sellers scrutinize the buyer's funding and the conditions attached to the deferred payment.

ADVANCEDYou're underwriting a single-asset CV on a business the GP has owned for six years. What's different vs. a fresh buyout?

The information asymmetry inverts: the GP knows everything, you know the data room. So the diligence emphasis shifts to (1) why the upside remains — if the plan was so good, why wasn't it executed in the first hold; (2) entry basis vs. NAV — you're paying near carrying value, so the mark's credibility is the deal; (3) the GP's incentive shift with reset economics — a new carry ratchet on an already-marked-up basis; (4) exit path realism — who buys this in five years at a bigger number, given the sponsor universe has already passed on it once at this price.

ADVANCEDWhy has the fairness-opinion share of GP-led volume fallen, and is that good or bad?

It dropped to roughly 11% of volume — buyers and LPACs increasingly treat a competitive process with multiple bids as better price discovery than a paid opinion. Arguably healthy: it reflects a deeper, more institutional buyer universe doing fundamental underwriting. The risk case: in thin processes with one anchor buyer, removing the third-party check leaves LP protection resting entirely on LPAC sophistication — which varies widely.

INTERMEDIATEWhat is the 'status quo option' debate?

ILPA guidance says rolling LPs should be offered a genuine status-quo option — rolling on their existing economic terms, not just the CV's new terms. GPs resist because reset economics are much of the point, and administering two term sets in one vehicle is messy. Many deals offer a modified roll (e.g., reduced fee for rollers) rather than true status quo. Knowing this debate signals you've followed the governance conversation.

INTERMEDIATEWho pays the transaction costs of a CV process, and why is that controversial?

Frequently the selling fund — meaning LPs, including those facing a forced choice, fund the advisor and legal costs of a process the GP initiated partly for its own benefit. ILPA pushes for disclosure and fair allocation; buyers increasingly negotiate cost-sharing. Small dollars, big optics: who bears the cost of resolving the GP's conflict?

INTERMEDIATEWhat happens to the GP's carried interest at a CV closing, and what's the tax nuance?

The carry crystallizes — the sale is a realization event, so accrued carry becomes payable. The alignment ask is that the GP rolls it into the CV; but crystallization can trigger tax for the GP's partners even when rolled, depending on structure, so rollover mechanics get negotiated hard. Likewise, 'rolling' LPs aren't automatically tax-deferred — an LP electing to continue can still recognize gain depending on how the roll is papered.

INTERMEDIATEWhy is warranty & indemnity (RWI) insurance increasingly used in GP-leds?

Because the seller and the buyer's future manager are the same person. A traditional indemnity from the selling fund means LPs — including rolling ones — backstop claims: circular. RWI shifts breach risk to an insurer, cleans the closing, and lets the GP give fuller representations than it otherwise would.

ADVANCEDWhat legal effect does LPAC approval of a CV conflict actually have?

Less than assumed. LPAC consent satisfies the contractual conflict-clearance mechanism in the LPA — strong process protection — but it doesn't immunize against every fiduciary theory (where duties aren't fully waived), doesn't bless the price, and LPAC members act in their own interest with exculpation, not as fiduciaries for other LPs. That gap between contractual clearance and underlying duty is where the legal debate lives — and why documenting a genuinely competitive process matters even after sign-off.

ADVANCEDWhere does US regulation of adviser-led secondaries stand?

The SEC's 2023 Private Fund Adviser rules would have required a fairness or valuation opinion for every adviser-led secondary — vacated by the Fifth Circuit in June 2024. No federal opinion mandate exists today; what governs is the Advisers Act's antifraud and fiduciary framework, the LPA, and ILPA norms. Candidates who say 'the SEC requires a fairness opinion' reveal stale knowledge.

ADVANCEDWhat is the 'CV-squared' problem?

As CV volume compounds, who buys these assets at the next exit? If the answer is increasingly 'another continuation vehicle' — second-generation CVs already exist — the market risks becoming self-referential: assets passing between vehicles at GP-influenced marks without a true third-party clearing price. Buyers underwrite this by demanding credible strategic/IPO exit paths, not 'we'll roll it again.'

ADVANCEDHow does a dual-track process (M&A vs. CV) change buyer behavior?

Sponsors increasingly run both simultaneously, each as a floor for the other. Buyers hate being the stalking horse: full single-asset underwriting costs real money and weeks, and losing to a strategic burns it. Responses: earlier exclusivity, expense reimbursement or break fees for the lead, or declining processes without commitment signals.

ADVANCEDHow is a large single-asset CV actually syndicated?

A lead (or co-leads) underwrites, sets price and terms, takes the biggest allocation; the advisor fills the book with syndicate investors accepting the lead's terms on lighter diligence. The lead earns governance rights and influence for underwriting risk; the syndicate trades influence for access. On $5B+ deals no single fund's concentration limits hold it — mega-SACVs only became possible once dedicated pools deepened the base.

ADVANCEDA GP marks an asset at 12x EBITDA and runs a CV at that NAV; public comps trade at 9x. How do you think about it?

Don't assume the mark is wrong — ask what justifies the premium: growth, margin trajectory, revenue quality, control premium. Then test it: if the CV clears with multiple credible bidders, the process validates the mark; if it's one anchor plus price-taking syndicate, the comp gap is a red flag. And cap your exit multiple at or below entry — paying 12x while modeling a 13x exit into 9x comps is how bad CVs get done.
