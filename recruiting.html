MODULE 09

The Recruiting Machine

On-cycle: the sprint

  • What it is: mega-fund and upper-middle-market PE recruiting for associate seats starting two years in the future, aimed at first-year banking analysts.
  • How it works: headhunters open registration and coffee chats weeks or months before "kickoff night." Then one evening — historically triggered by one fund jumping — interviews launch and offers go out within 24–72 hours. Candidates do back-to-back modeling tests overnight.
  • The creep: kickoff has moved absurdly early — recent cycles launched within a few months of analysts hitting the desk. Timing shifts every year and firms periodically threaten to slow it down; treat any specific date you read as stale and verify with current-cycle chatter.
  • What decides it: preparation done before your banking training ends — paper LBOs cold, a 2-hour LBO model test rep'd 10+ times, deal stories rehearsed, headhunter datasheets polished.

Off-cycle: the marathon (and where most great seats actually are)

  • Middle-market PE, growth equity, secondaries, co-investment platforms, credit, and most hedge funds hire on their own timelines, usually for near-term starts.
  • Processes run 3–8 weeks: screens → technicals → case study or take-home model → investment committee-style final rounds.
  • Networking matters far more here — direct outreach to team members, warm referrals from counterparties you've worked across, and staying visibly excellent in your current seat.

Playing the headhunter meeting correctly

  • They are screens, not advocates — paid by funds, ranking you from minute one. Treat every "intro chat" as a first-round interview.
  • Have a tight answer on: preferred strategy (be specific — "GP-led secondaries and co-invest platforms" beats "open to anything"), geography, fund size, and 2–3 named target firms with reasons.
  • Fill their datasheets fast and flawlessly. GPA, test scores, deal experience — they filter mechanically before anyone reads your story.
  • Never bluff readiness. If you say you're ready, a model test can arrive that week.

The three ways candidates die

1. Technicals fine, story weak — no coherent reason for this strategy at this firm. 2. Deal-experience bluffing — you claimed the model was yours and got dismantled two questions deep. 3. Slow arithmetic — paper LBOs and mental math are pass/fail. All three are fixable with reps, none are fixable in the room.

MODULE 09

The Interview Arsenal

Mental math you must own

  • Percent shortcuts: 15% of 240 = 10% (24) + 5% (12) = 36. Reverse: 36/240 → 36/24 = 1.5 → 15%.
  • Multiples: 7 × 13, 18 × 25, squares to 25², halves and doubles (18×25 = 9×50 = 450).
  • Compounding anchors: 1.1⁵ ≈ 1.61; 1.15⁵ ≈ 2.0; 1.2⁵ ≈ 2.5; 1.25⁵ ≈ 3.05. These four unlock most paper LBOs.
  • Rule of 72 for doubling; roughly Rule of 114 for tripling.

Technical question bank

What are the three drivers of LBO returns, ranked by quality?

(1) EBITDA growth — highest quality, repeatable, what LPs pay for. (2) Deleveraging — mechanical but real; it's the company buying itself for you. (3) Multiple expansion — lowest quality; you're betting on market mood or re-rating. Sophisticated follow-up: multiple expansion can be earned (mix shift to recurring revenue, scale past a size threshold) rather than lucked into — distinguish the two.

Higher purchase price or higher leverage — which hurts IRR more?

Frame with math: paying one extra turn of EBITDA raises the equity check (or debt) dollar-for-dollar and directly compresses MOIC. Extra leverage cuts the equity check, which raises IRR if the deal works — leverage amplifies both directions. So overpaying always hurts; leverage hurts only in the downside. The follow-up they want: leverage increases the probability-weighted cost via financial distress and covenant risk.

Walk me through a dividend recap and its effect on returns.

Portfolio company issues new debt and pays the proceeds to the sponsor as a dividend. MOIC on remaining equity is unchanged-to-lower, but IRR typically rises because capital comes back sooner, and downside is truncated (you've de-risked the basis). Trade-off: higher leverage on the company, less cushion. Common two or three years in when performance is ahead of plan.

Company has $100 EBITDA, bought at 10x with 6x leverage. EBITDA drops 30%. Where does the equity stand?

Entry: $1,000 EV, $600 debt, $400 equity. New EBITDA $70. If the market still values it at 10x → $700 EV − ~$600 debt = $100 equity: down 75% on a 30% EBITDA decline. That asymmetry — leverage turning operational bruises into equity wounds — is the whole risk lecture in one example. If the multiple also compresses to 8x: $560 EV, equity is wiped.

How does buying an LP interest at 90% of NAV translate into returns?

The 10-point discount is an instant uplift (buy at 90, marked at 100 → ~1.11x day one), but real returns come from what the NAV does: future company growth, exit timing, and remaining unfunded calls. Underwrite the assets to exit values, model the distribution pacing, then solve what price hits your target IRR — the discount is the output of that math, not the thesis.

Merger arb: target trades at $48 after a $50 all-cash offer. What's the trade and the math?

Buy at $48; if the deal closes you earn $2/$48 ≈ 4.2% gross — annualize by time-to-close (6 months → ~8.5%). The spread prices deal-break risk: if the stock falls to $40 on a break, the implied probability of closing at these prices is p·50 + (1−p)·40 = 48 → p = 80%. Your edge must be a view that true probability exceeds what's priced (antitrust read, financing certainty, vote math).

Pitch me a stock (the structure that works).

One line of business description → variant perception ("the market thinks X; I think Y because Z") → 2–3 catalysts with timing → valuation: base/bull/bear with probabilities → key risks and what would make you exit → sizing. 3 minutes max. The most common failure: a good company description with no reason the mispricing exists.

Why might a company with negative working capital be attractive?

It collects cash before paying suppliers (subscriptions, insurance float, some retail) — growth generates cash instead of consuming it. In an LBO, that self-funding dynamic accelerates debt paydown. The check: is it structural (business model) or squeezed (stretching payables unsustainably)?

The modeling test — how to prepare

  1. Build a 1-hour LBO from blank Excel until you can do it in 45 minutes: S&U → mini operating model → debt schedule with cash sweep → returns → sensitivities.
  2. Build a 3-hour version with revolver mechanics, multiple tranches, management rollover, and a returns waterfall.
  3. Keyboard only. Alt-shortcuts, F4 anchoring, consistent color coding (blue inputs, black formulas).
  4. When told the test is "open format," they're testing judgment: state assumptions in a box, keep it clean, finish with a one-paragraph recommendation. A complete simple model beats an unfinished complex one, every time.

Behavioral round — the six you must have cold

  • Walk me through your resume (2 minutes, ends with why this seat).
  • Walk me through your most significant deal — and be ready for five levels of "why" on the numbers.
  • Why this strategy / why this firm (specific, evidence-based, not flattery).
  • A time you disagreed with someone senior; a mistake and what changed after it.
  • An investment you'd make with your own money (have one long and one skeptical view).
  • Questions for them — ask about underwriting process, a recent deal's thesis, how juniors get repetitions at bat.
MODULE 09

Telling Your Story

Technicals get you to par. The story is what separates offers from callbacks — and it has an architecture.

The spine: thread, proof, destination

  • Thread: one sentence that makes your path look intentional. Every job, class, and project should hang off it.
  • Proof: the 2–3 experiences that show you've already done a junior version of the job — a deal you can defend to five levels of depth, a model you built, a view you formed that turned out to matter.
  • Destination: why this seat is the logical next chapter — named team, named strategy, named reason. "I want to be an investor" is table stakes; "I want to underwrite GP-led transactions from the buy side because I've spent a year watching how buyers price them from the sell side" is a story.

Deal walkthrough format (the STAR of finance)

  1. Context: company, size, situation, your team's role — 20 seconds.
  2. Your work: the specific analyses you touched. Own only what you can defend.
  3. The tension: what was hard, contested, or surprising — this is where interviews are won.
  4. The view: would you have done the deal? At what price? What did the process teach you about how buyers think?
Delivery notes

Write answers as spoken scripts, not essays — short sentences, natural connectors, numbers you can say out loud. Rehearse until you can compress each answer to 60% of its length on demand; senior interviewers reward candidates who can land the point and stop talking.

Case-study formats to expect

  • LP portfolio pricing case: given a 10–30 line portfolio (fund, vintage, NAV, unfunded, strategy), triage the book, price the top names bottom-up, top-down the tail, and land a % of NAV with an IRR bridge — discount accretion + NAV growth + timing. They’re testing triage judgment, not spreadsheet heroics.
  • Single-asset CV case: a mini-CIM on one company at a stated NAV — underwrite to exit, opine on the mark, propose structure (price vs. NAV, deferral, carry tiers, GP roll) and name the three diligence items that would change your answer. The alignment section is where candidates separate.
  • Paper LBO / quick math: entry-exit MOIC/IRR arithmetic under time pressure — the Rule of 72 and the MOIC-to-IRR ladder (2.0x/5yr ≈ 15%; 2.0x/4yr ≈ 19%; 1.5x/3yr ≈ 14%) should be reflexive.
  • Portfolio-construction discussion: how would you deploy a $2B fund across LP-led, GP-led, and structured? Pacing, concentration limits, and the honesty to say what you’d avoid at today’s pricing.
